## 1. The Big Picture

### What the business does

Piers Day is a clinical hypnotherapist and therapist with 27 years in practice, based in Bury St Edmunds, Suffolk. The work helps people out of anxiety, depression, worry, fear, phobias, dark thoughts, self-harm and suicidal thinking. Somewhere between 2,000 and 3,000 people have been seen over that time.

The method is metaphor-led hypnotherapy with direct suggestion built in, refined through years of training with leading practitioners around the world. Sessions run face to face (clinic in Bury St Edmunds, or at home) and online, which works just as well and brings in clients from all over the world.

### Why it exists

Piers came into this work after friends took their own lives. That loss is the reason the work exists, and it is why anxiety, depression and suicidal thinking are the heart of the practice rather than a sideline. The work is not experienced as work. Helping young people is the part that matters most.

### History

Late 1990s to around 2012: best known for weight control (healthy choices without dieting, ending binge eating and cravings for sweet and fatty foods). Stop smoking has always been a strength. From about 2012 the focus moved to anxiety, worry and depression. Since COVID, and through a period of cancer treatment, hours were reduced, but the practice kept running. Health has improved over the last three months and the diary has filled back up.

### Credentials and training

Original training: Cambridge College of Hypnotherapy.
Further training with Mike Mandel and Cal Banyan, among others, in hypnosis.
NLP Master Practitioner. Reiki Master. EMDR trained.
No current professional membership (dropped on cost). See Guardrails for why rejoining one register is worth it now.

The method: metaphor-led hypnotherapy with direct suggestion built in. Metaphors are the core tool, and a library of them is planned as a product (see Offers).

### The one-sentence position

Piers Day helps teenagers and young men get through the hardest stretch of their lives, the move from school to university to the real world, so their families get them back.

### The five-year ambition

Become the number one name in the UK for helping teenagers and young men through the transition from school to university to adult life. Piers is 64 and wants to retire in five years, so the business has to grow through things that sell and spread without Piers in the room: card decks, recordings, the podcast, retreats, and schools.

### The problem being solved

Young men are struggling, and it has spread right up the social scale. Many in their early 20s have done everything asked of them, got the grades and the degree, and cannot find work. The frustration comes out at the people they love and inward as depression, with weed, cocaine or alcohol used to get through the day. The NHS route is slow (GP to MIND, six to eight month waiting lists) and leans heavily on medication. Parents often do not realise how much their child is suffering until it is very late.

### The Living Game range

Family Edition: ready for print, 55 cards (per the box specification).

More age bands to follow.
The Forgotten Forties: for people aged 40 to mid-50s who find it hard to talk about their problems with family or friends. Played after dinner with trusted friends or round the family table.

Margins: £25 retail less £6.50 unit cost leaves about £18.50 before postage and payment fees. Trade at £15 leaves about £8.50.

### The retreat

Weekend format, three days. Small country hotel, one large room hired. Participants arrange their own food and accommodation (stay in the hotel or locally). Piers provides tea, coffee and water. Content: visualisation, meditation, practical techniques and journaling, aimed at clearing old limiting beliefs and negative instructions so people can succeed and feel at home in their own skin.

Economics at £1,200 per person: 12 people brings in £14,400; 15 brings in £18,000. Costs under £2,500 (room around £250 to £300 a day, refreshments around £10 to £12 per person per day, plus Piers's own accommodation). Net per retreat roughly £12,000 to £15,500.

Decision: start at 18+. Under-18s bring safeguarding, consent, ratio and insurance requirements that a solo practitioner should not take on. The 18 to 25 young man in transition is the target client anyway, and his parents will often pay. A parents' retreat or a Forgotten Forties retreat can follow.

### How to sell packages properly

Past packages failed because they were pitched as a discount. Worried parents hear "buy more, save money" as being sold to. Frame it as a plan instead:

"Most young people I see need four to six sessions. We meet weekly for the first few, then I leave a gap of two or three weeks before the last one so we can check everything is holding. Here is what we work on at each stage."

Then offer the plan as a single price (for example, six sessions for £720 against £770 paid separately), with the option to pay in two or three instalments. Introduce the monthly maintenance option at the start, as part of the plan ("and then once a month for six months to make it stick"), not at the end when the family already feels fixed. A one-page PDF explaining the plan is on the to-do list.

## 4. The Customers

### Who pays and who is helped

The buyer is usually a middle-class mother or father. The client is usually their son, aged roughly 14 to 25, sometimes a daughter. Parents want their children to do as well as they possibly can, and they are scared.

### The core client

A young man between school and adult life. Anxious or low, spending most of his time in his bedroom, not mixing with his year group. In the older band: degree done, no job, frustrated, taking it out on family, using weed, cocaine or alcohol to cope. Some have undisclosed autism or ADHD; mild to moderate cases respond well. Often, what they need most is someone to talk to who does not judge them.

### What parents say on the first call (their words)

"I'm worried about my son."
"I've tried to get help elsewhere. The doctor keeps referring me to MIND, and the waiting list is six to eight months."
"He's had a massive panic attack and spends most of his time in his bedroom. He doesn't want to go out and mix with his year group."
"You are my last hope. I've discovered my son has been self-harming and I have no idea how to deal with it."
"He wishes he'd never been born, his life is so terrible."

### What parents say afterwards (their words)

"He's back on form, like his old self again. He's going out with his friends, he's a pleasure to be around, and we're enjoying family time again."
"It's so nice to have my son back. I thought I'd lost him."
"Since the last session I've been able to sleep soundly again, knowing all my family are well." (a father, about his daughter)

Action: ask these clients for permission to quote them by first name and town.

### How they find Piers

Mostly recommendations from other parents. Second, young people hear about Piers at school from a friend who has been helped. Social media, the podcast and the two websites play a smaller role today.

### Seasonality

Busiest in March, April and May (exam season). Steady flow the rest of the year.

### Why people do not book

Money is the main reason given. The deeper problem is that many parents do not realise how much their child is suffering until late. Response: offer to spread payment across the sessions or in instalments, because most "we cannot afford it" means "we cannot find £800 this month." Earlier education (talks, cards, podcast) reaches parents before the crisis.

### The intake process (do not change the spirit of this)

1. Parent calls. Piers asks what has brought them in touch, then listens properly. Parents pay, so they get serious listening time. Their picture often differs from the child's.
2. Piers explains what can be done and how past clients have recovered. No selling.
3. A five to ten minute call with the young person, purely to hear their side and check they are suitable for this kind of work.
4. Where parental frustration is part of the picture, Piers may suggest a few sessions for the parent too.
5. Piers books the appointment and sends a manual invoice.

## 5. The Business Model

### Where the money comes from today

Last 12 months: about £45,000, during a period of illness and cancer treatment.
Last three months: 15 to 20 clients a week, about £2,000 a week. Over a 46-week year that is a run rate of roughly £92,000.
Stop smoking brings in one or two clients a month (roughly £7,000 to £14,000 a year). Everything else comes from therapy. Weight control has not been promoted and is rare. Cards, recordings, retreats and talks have not yet produced income.

### The target

At least £120,000 a year, with retreats, cards and speaking taking it well past that. Growth has to come from products and retreats rather than more hours, because health limits the diary to around 15 to 20 sessions a week and retirement is five years away.

### Where the growth is

1. Stop smoking is the fastest win. Every extra smoker is £575 for two hours, and four extra a month is only eight more hours a month. icanstopyousmoking.com plus local Google ads and a Google Business Profile can deliver that within months.
2. Retreats are the biggest single lever. One retreat earns about the same as 100 one-to-one sessions.
3. The Living Game through schools and therapists. Every deck in a school or therapy room is marketing Piers does not have to do, and it keeps selling after retirement.
4. The email list built from the cards and the free sleep recording. It turns one-off clients into a following that buys recordings, retreats and the next edition.
5. The podcast as the long-term trust builder for parents across the UK.

### The five-year exit path

Years 1 to 2: one-to-one stays at current levels, retreats launched, cards printed and in schools, list built.
Years 3 to 4: one-to-one hours start to fall; retreats, cards and recordings carry more of the income.
Year 5: the card range, recordings and podcast library are assets that keep earning, can be licensed to schools or therapists, or sold.

## 7. Guardrails

### Safeguarding and crisis

This practice works with people who talk about suicide and self-harm. Keep these numbers ready, and include them in written materials and on the websites:
Samaritans 116 123 (24 hours, free)
Papyrus HOPELINE247 0800 068 4141 (for under-35s and worried parents)
Shout, text SHOUT to 85258
NHS 111, option 2 for urgent mental health help in England
999 for immediate danger

Enhanced DBS check is in progress and due within a week. Get it before contacting any school.

### Membership and insurance

There is no current professional membership. Rejoining one accredited register (CNHC or the National Council for Hypnotherapy are the usual choices) is worth the annual fee now. Schools, PTAs and retreat venues will ask, it backs up the advertising claims the ASA expects, and it reassures a frightened parent in a single line. Confirm professional indemnity and public liability insurance covers group work, school talks and retreats before booking any of them.

### Advertising rules

Before any website, social or ad copy goes public, check it against the ASA and CAP guidance for hypnotherapy. That guidance restricts claims to treat medical conditions such as depression and expects practitioners not to discourage people from seeking medical help. Client quotes describing how they feel are safer than claims to cure.

### Educating people about hypnosis

Fear of the word is a real barrier to booking. Build a steady drip of plain, calm explanation on piersday.com and the podcast: what a session actually feels like, that the client stays aware and in control throughout, that nobody can be made to do anything against their will, and how it differs from stage hypnosis. A short FAQ page, one podcast episode, and a handful of short videos answering the questions parents ask most would cover it. Parents who understand it stop being afraid of it.

### Tone

Calm, warm, direct, non-judgemental. Never pushy. The listening-first intake is the brand.
